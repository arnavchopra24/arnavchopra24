Union Pacific runs trains across the western U.S. Norfolk Southern runs them across the East. Freight crossing the country gets handed from one railroad to another along the way. In July 2025, Union Pacific agreed to buy Norfolk Southern so it could run the whole trip itself.

The deal was announced at $320 a share. What I found most interesting is that the number has changed every day since.

## How the offer works

Norfolk shareholders get $88.82 in cash plus one Union Pacific share. So the offer is worth $88.82 plus whatever Union Pacific's stock is worth that day. Union Pacific's shares have risen since the signing, which pushed the offer up too.

## The gap the market is pricing

At the Nov. 9 close, Union Pacific was at $297.01, so the offer was worth about $385.83 a share. Norfolk closed at $325.82.

That gap of about $60 is called the merger spread. If investors were sure the deal would close, they would bid Norfolk up close to the offer's value. They haven't, which tells me the market sees a real chance regulators say no.

## Why the regulator matters

Railroad mergers need approval from the Surface Transportation Board, and this is the first big deal judged under the stricter rules it adopted in 2001. Rivals BNSF and CSX, along with some shipper groups, have asked the board to reject it.

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| Jul 29, 2025 | Deal announced |
| Nov 14, 2025 | Shareholders of both companies vote yes |
| Dec 2025 | First application filed, later rejected as incomplete |
| Apr 30, 2026 | Revised application filed |
| May 28, 2026 | Board accepts it but asks for more information |
| Aug 18, 2026 | Review restarts with a schedule |
| Nov 18, 2026 | Comments from opponents due |
| Feb 16, 2027 | Railroads' responses due |
| May 28, 2027 | Final briefs due; decision within 90 days after the record closes |

## Why Union Pacific wants it

Based on 2024 results, the combined railroad would have about $36 billion of revenue and $18 billion of EBITDA. The companies expect $2.75 billion of yearly synergies, and Norfolk shareholders would end up owning about 27% of the combined company.

## My take

The strategy makes sense to me: one railroad from coast to coast means fewer handoffs, which should make rail more competitive with trucks. But the price only matters if regulators approve the deal, and a 16% gap says that's far from certain. For now, it's too early to tell.

## What this deal taught me

- In a cash-and-stock deal, the headline price is only a snapshot of signing day.
- The merger spread is the market's own guess at whether a deal will close.
