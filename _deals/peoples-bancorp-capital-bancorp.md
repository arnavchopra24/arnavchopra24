## Deal Snapshot

Peoples Bancorp, a $9.5 billion bank based in Marietta, Ohio, agreed on 30 September to buy Capital Bancorp of Rockville, Maryland, which has $3.9 billion in assets, in an all-stock deal worth $728.1 million. Capital shareholders get 1.11 Peoples shares for each share they own, worth $43.75 at Peoples' 20-day average price of $39.41, and will end up owning about 32% of the combined bank. The deal is expected to close in the first half of 2027.

Capital is not a typical community bank. Alongside a commercial bank in the Washington, D.C. and Baltimore markets, it runs three national businesses:

- **OpenSky**, credit cards sold online to people building or rebuilding their credit
- **Windsor Advantage**, which services SBA and USDA loans for other lenders, with a portfolio of about $3.4 billion
- **Capital Bank Home Loans**, a mortgage business

Fees made up about 22% of Capital's revenue in the second quarter.

## Valuation

Peoples is paying 1.91x Capital's tangible book value, 12.9x its last twelve months of earnings, and a 22.7% premium to its share price the day before. That is a full price. In April, Peoples paid 1.20x tangible book for Citizens Bank of Kentucky.

What makes the price expensive for Peoples' own shareholders is the comparison with its own stock. Peoples was trading at roughly 1.7x its tangible book, based on its 20-day average price and its 31 March tangible book of $22.95 a share. Because it is paying for Capital in shares, buying at 1.9x with currency worth 1.7x means issuing more book value than it receives. That gap is where the tangible book dilution below comes from.

The case for paying up is that Capital is not a plain-vanilla bank. Its specialty businesses grow faster and earn more in fees than a typical community bank. Peoples is targeting a return on tangible common equity of about 20% for the combined company.

## Strategic Rationale

**1. Clearing $10 billion in one move.** Crossing $10 billion in assets brings a cap on debit card interchange fees (the Durbin Amendment) and supervision by the CFPB. Peoples estimates the fee cap alone will cost it about $11 million a year before tax, including Citizens. Earlier this year it planned to sell Citizens' securities portfolio to stay under the line. This deal does the opposite: it jumps straight to about $14 billion, so the new costs land on a bigger earnings base.

Capital barely adds to that cost. Peoples' presentation puts Capital's share of the Durbin hit at under $50,000, because Capital's revenue comes from credit cards, loan servicing and lending rather than debit card fees.

**2. Faster-growing, fee-heavy businesses.** OpenSky, Windsor Advantage and the mortgage business are national rather than tied to Peoples' branches in Ohio, West Virginia and Kentucky. CEO Tyler Wilcox has said he expects Capital's historically higher growth rates to continue inside Peoples.

**3. A bigger foothold around Washington.** Peoples entered the D.C. area through a 2021 acquisition. Capital's commercial bank deepens that presence and adds Baltimore.

## Capital and Accretion

Peoples' presentation shows about **19% accretion to 2027 earnings per share** and **10.8% dilution to tangible book value per share**, earned back in **under three years**, with an internal rate of return **above 25%**. The deal also carries $56.5 million of one-time pre-tax costs and a 3.0% credit mark on Capital's loans, about $93 million.

{% include bar-chart.html chart=page.charts.math %}

An earnback under three years is the line most bank investors use to call a deal acceptable, so Peoples clears it. But the 19% figure assumes cost savings are fully in place for all of 2027. On its October earnings call, management said they won't be until 2028. Accretion in the first full year will therefore be lower than the headline. I could not find the pro forma CET1 ratio in public sources, so I have left it out rather than estimate it.

## Cost Savings

Peoples expects to cut costs equal to 30% of Capital's noninterest expense, with most of the savings in 2027 and all of them by 2028. That looks achievable to me. Peoples targeted 40% savings on Citizens, an in-market deal in Kentucky, where it already has branches. Capital has only a handful of branches, and its specialty businesses need their own staff, so most of the savings have to come from back-office systems and the costs of being a separate public company.

## Risks

- **Consumer credit.** OpenSky lends to people with thin or damaged credit. Credit cards are only about 5% of Capital's loans but produced about $20 million of revenue in the second quarter, roughly 31% of Capital's total. High yields come with high losses. Capital's nonperforming assets also rose to 1.56% of assets in the second quarter, from 1.07% a year earlier. The 3% credit mark shows Peoples is taking that seriously.
- **Two integrations at once.** Peoples' Citizens deal was due to close on 30 October, so Capital will be the second integration in about six months, and its cost savings come later than the headline math assumes.
- **Costs arrive before the payoff.** The Durbin cap and the heavier regulation that comes with $10 billion start once Peoples crosses the line. If Capital's businesses slow down, Peoples carries those costs without the earnings that were meant to cover them.
- **Approvals.** Both companies' shareholders and the bank regulators still have to sign off.

## Market Reaction

The market didn't like the deal for Peoples. Its shares fell about 5.3% to $36.80 on announcement day, while Capital's rose about 11.7% to $39.80. Because this is a stock deal, Peoples' drop came straight out of what Capital holders receive. At $36.80, 1.11 Peoples shares are worth about $40.85, almost $3 below the headline $43.75. Capital traded about 2.6% below that, a normal gap for a deal that won't close until 2027.

## My Take

Too early to tell, though I lean positive on the strategy. Crossing $10 billion was coming either way, and doing it with a bank that brings fee income and growth, while adding almost nothing to the Durbin bill, is better than drifting across the line. The price is full, but the deal clears the usual tests: double-digit EPS accretion, earnback under three years and an IRR above 25%.

What I can't judge yet is OpenSky. An Ohio community bank is taking on a national consumer credit card business that lends to riskier borrowers, at the same time as it integrates another acquisition. The things I'll watch:

- OpenSky's charge-offs
- Whether 2027 cost savings arrive on schedule
- Whether Peoples' share price recovers once investors see the combined results

## 30-Second Version

Peoples Bancorp, a $9.5 billion Ohio bank, is buying Capital Bancorp in Maryland for $728 million in stock. That's 1.9x tangible book and a 23% premium. The real reason is the $10 billion threshold. Peoples was going to cross it anyway, and this gets it to $14 billion in one step, with fee-heavy national businesses that barely add to the debit-fee cap. The math works on paper: about 19% EPS accretion and 10.8% tangible book dilution, earned back in under three years. But the market sent Peoples' stock down 5%, the cost savings don't fully arrive until 2028, and the big unknown is the credit risk in Capital's OpenSky card business. So I'd call it too early to tell, leaning positive.
